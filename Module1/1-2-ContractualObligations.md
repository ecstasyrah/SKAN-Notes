## Why External Regulation Matters

An organization's **risk assessment** helps determine which technical security controls it needs.

However, **risk is not the only concern**.

Organizations do not operate in isolation. They interact with an external environment that includes:

* Laws
* Regulations
* Customers

Organizations must therefore consider external requirements when deciding how to manage information security.

---

# Laws and Regulations

Organizations already need to understand laws and regulations related to their normal business activities, such as:

* Paying taxes
* Employment laws
* Health and safety regulations

They also need to understand laws and regulations that affect **information security**.

Some countries have laws and regulations that specifically tell organizations what information security measures they should have.

These requirements are based on the risks that the **legislative body** believes are relevant to society.

---

# What Is a Legislative Body?

A **legislative body** is a governmental organization that has the authority to create laws that an organization is required to follow.

It can exist at different levels:

* **Supranational level** — such as the European Union
* **National level** — a country's government
* **State level**
* **Local level**

---

# Three Categories of Information Security Laws and Regulations

The discussion identifies three main areas where information security laws and regulations can apply.

## 1. Sector-Specific Requirements

These requirements focus on particular industries or sectors.

### Example: HIPAA

**HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)**

This is concerned with **healthcare information** in the United States.

### Example: EU Security of Network and Information Systems Directive

This addresses cybersecurity of **essential services**, including areas such as:

* Transport
* Banking
* Energy

### Example: U.S. Presidential Executive Order

The **U.S. Presidential Executive Order on Improving the Nation's Cybersecurity** introduced security rules and objectives for federal entities.

---

## 2. Data Protection and Privacy

The second area is:

**Data protection and privacy**

One of the best-known examples is:

### GDPR

**General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**

It is an EU regulation concerning data protection and privacy.

Many other countries also have similar laws.

In the United States, individual states have implemented privacy laws that include security requirements.

Examples mentioned in the discussion include:

* California Consumer Privacy Act
* Utah Consumer Privacy Act
* Connecticut Data Privacy Act

---

## 3. Cybersecurity of Physical Devices

The third area concerns the cybersecurity of **physical devices**.

For example, the U.S. **Food and Drug Administration (FDA)** has rules concerning the cybersecurity of medical devices.

The:

* European Union
* United Kingdom
* United States

also have developing laws concerning the cybersecurity of **IoT devices**.

---

# Laws and Regulations Can Have Different Names

The speaker uses the phrase **laws and regulations** as a general term for things that an organization is legally required to comply with.

Different countries and states may use different names for their legislative instruments.

Therefore, if an organization needs to determine exactly what it is legally required to do, its **legal team** can help identify the applicable requirements.

---

# Laws Can Be Vague About Information Security

Information security laws are often relatively general.

For example, a law might require an organization to have:

* Appropriate security
* Reasonable security

rather than specifying every individual technical security measure.

### Why?

Technology and threats change frequently.

However:

> **Laws cannot change as quickly as technology and threats.**

Because of this, laws often provide general requirements rather than detailed technical instructions.

---

# Role of Regulators

A **regulator** may be responsible for enforcing a law.

Regulators can publish:

* Frameworks
* Standards
* Guidance

These can help organizations understand what they need to do to meet the more general requirements of a law.

---

# Compliance — The "C" in GRC

This is where we meet the **C in GRC: Compliance**.

Compliance helps answer:

> **What information security does the organization actually need to do in order to comply with external requirements?**

An organization may need to consider a combination of:

* Laws
* Regulations
* Frameworks
* Standards
* Guidance

---

# Role of the Compliance Manager

The **compliance manager** works closely with the **legal team**.

They need to understand:

* Which external laws apply
* Which regulations apply
* Which guidelines apply
* Whether the requirements apply to the entire IT environment
* Whether they apply only to specific parts of the organization's IT systems

---

# Comparing Requirements with Existing Controls

After identifying the external requirements, the compliance manager considers the organization's existing information security controls.

These controls are already defined or required by the organizations:

**Information Security Policy**

The compliance manager then determines:

> **Is the organization currently compliant with the external requirements?**

If the organization is not compliant, changes to the information security policy may need to be proposed.

---

# External Standards and Frameworks

Regulators may sometimes refer organizations to external standards and frameworks.

One example mentioned is:

**ISO 27001**

Another example mentioned in the discussion is the:

**NIST Cybersecurity Framework**

These standards and frameworks can provide a baseline for information security.

---

# Relationship Between Risk and Compliance

Sometimes an organization may already have a **risk-based decision** to adopt a particular standard or framework.

If this happens, the requirements from:

**Risk**

and

**Compliance**

may become very similar or even identical.

For example, if an organization has already chosen a widely used cybersecurity framework as its security baseline, that same framework may help satisfy both:

* The organization's risk requirements
* External compliance requirements

---

# Legal Defensibility

Internal legal departments may also use widely accepted standards or frameworks as a baseline.

This is especially useful when a law or regulation uses general terms such as:

> **"Reasonable security"**

or

> **"Appropriate security"**

Using a widely recognized standard or framework as a baseline can provide a **legally defensible position** for the organization.

---

The overall idea can be understood as:

---

# Main Idea

> **Risk assessment determines security controls based on the risks an organization faces, but external laws, regulations, and contractual demands can also determine what security an organization needs to implement.**

The **Compliance** part of GRC helps the organization understand its external requirements and determine whether its existing information security controls meet those requirements.

Because laws are often general, **regulators, standards, frameworks, and guidance** help organizations understand what "reasonable" or "appropriate" information security should look like.

# Contractual Requirements

## Contracts Can Affect Information Security

External laws and regulations are **not the only things** that can determine what information security an organization needs.

Organizations often sign **contracts with customers** that contain information security requirements.

Therefore, an organization needs to consider:

* Laws
* Regulations
* Customer contracts
* Security standards required by contracts

---

### 1. Contracts Using "Appropriate" or "Reasonable" Security

Some contracts may use general terms such as:

* **Appropriate security**
* **Reasonable security**
* **Good practice**

When this happens, the required security should generally be reflected in the information security controls already determined through the organization's **risk assessment**.

The **legal team** can advise the organization on how these contractual requirements should be interpreted.

---

### 2. Contracts With Specific Security Requirements

Other contracts may be more specific.

A contract might:

* Specify particular security controls that the customer expects
* Require the organization to comply with the customer's own security standard
* Require compliance with a published industry security standard

These requirements become commitments that the organization needs to meet.

---

#### Examples of Industry Security Standards

The discussion gives several examples.

1. PCI DSS

**Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)**

This applies to organizations involved in the **payment card ecosystem**.

2. SWIFT Customer Security Controls Framework

This is relevant to organizations that want to connect to the:

**SWIFT Global Payments network**

The organization may need to comply with SWIFT's security controls.

3. Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification

**Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC)**

This applies to suppliers in the:

**U.S. defense industrial base**

---

# Role of the Compliance Manager

The **compliance manager** works closely with the **legal team** to understand:

> **What has the organization committed to through its contracts?**

They determine:

* What security requirements are included in the contract
* What standards the organization has agreed to follow
* Whether the organization's existing controls meet those requirements

---

# Does the Information Security Policy Need to Change?

After reviewing the contractual requirements, the compliance manager determines whether the organization's:

**Information Security Policy**

needs to be changed.

If the existing controls do not meet the contractual requirements, changes may need to be made.

---

# Compliance Risk and Interpretation

## Two Other Aspects of Compliance

When dealing with the external environment, there are two additional questions:

1. **How should external security requirements be interpreted?**
2. **Will the organization comply with those requirements?**

Compliance managers and compliance analysts play an important role in answering both questions.

---

## Interpreting External Requirements

Organizations can be given information security requirements in many different ways.

Because of this, compliance specialists may need to consult **multiple documents** to understand exactly what is required.

The compliance team's role is not only to determine what should be included in the **information security policy**.

They also advise:

* Security engineering
* Security architecture

on how to implement the requirements in a **compliant way**.

---

## Multiple Sources May Need to Be Considered

Understanding a compliance requirement can be like **peeling back the layers of an onion**.

A compliance team may need to look beyond the original regulation or contract and also consider:

* Standards
* Guidance
* FAQs
* Previous examples of regulatory enforcement

These additional sources can help explain how a requirement should be understood and implemented.

---

## Compliance and Risk Assessment Work Together

External compliance requirements cannot be considered separately from the organization's own **risk assessment**.

The organization needs to combine:

**External requirements**

with

**Risk assessment decisions**

This helps determine the appropriate level of protection for a particular:

* System
* Application

So compliance requirements and risk-based security decisions need to work together.

---

## Can an Organization Choose Not to Fully Comply?

The discussion then asks an important question:

> **Can an organization simply ignore applicable laws, regulations, or contractual obligations and do whatever security it wants?**

The obvious reaction is:

> **No.**

However, the speaker explains that organizations can make **risk-based decisions about their degree of compliance**.

---

### Question:

> Do you always drive within the speed limit?

People might answer:

> "Sometimes" or "Usually."

When someone decides to drive faster than the speed limit, they may consider the **risks** involved.

They might consider:

* How likely they are to be caught
    * Whether there is a police officer
    * Whether there is a speed camera
* What the penalty would be
* Road conditions
* Weather
* Amount of traffic
* Likelihood of an accident

This is essentially a **risk-based decision**.

---

## Organizations Make Similar Decisions

Organizations can make similar decisions regarding:

**Regulatory and compliance risk**

As information security professionals, we might not agree with an organization's decision to accept compliance risk.

However, the speaker explains that:

> **The degree of compliance can be a legitimate business decision.**

Organizations have many competing considerations, so they may evaluate the risk associated with not fully meeting a requirement.

---

## Role of Compliance Managers

Compliance managers help **risk managers** understand and explain compliance risk.

They help determine:

* What the organization is required to do
* How far the organization is from those requirements
* What could happen if the organization does not comply
* How likely the non-compliance is to be discovered

---

## Compliance Risk

Like other types of risk, compliance risk has two components:

### 1. Likelihood

How likely is the compliance problem to result in consequences?

The speaker explains that likelihood depends on two things:

- **a. Degree of Deviation**

How far does the organization's information security posture deviate from what:

* The regulator expects
* The contract requires

- **b. Probability of Discovery**

How likely is it that the deviation will be discovered?

Therefore:

> **Compliance risk likelihood depends on both how much the organization deviates from the requirement and how likely that deviation is to be discovered.**

### 2. Compliance Risk Impact

The **impact** is what could happen if the organization's non-compliance is discovered.

The impact can range from relatively low to very serious.

### Low Impact

For example:

* A regulator sends a disapproving letter

### High Impact

Potentially:

* Prohibition from continuing a business activity
* Large financial penalty
* Executives being arrested

The exact impact depends on the situation and applicable requirements.

---

## Regulatory Risk Decisions

Risk managers and risk analysts can use the:

* Likelihood
* Impact

of compliance risk to recommend an appropriate course of action.

Their recommendations should consider the organization's:

> **Regulatory risk tolerance**

The organization can then decide how it wants to manage the compliance risk.

---

## Regulators and Customers Also Manage Risk

Organizations are not the only ones making risk-based decisions.

Regulators and customers understand that organizations may make decisions about compliance risk.

Therefore, they have strategies to:

* **Deter** non-compliance
* **Detect** non-compliance

The speaker compares this to police using **speed cameras**.

Speed cameras can discourage people from speeding and can also detect people who choose to speed.

Similarly, regulators and customers can create mechanisms that discourage and identify compliance violations.

---

---

## Main Idea

> **Compliance is not always as simple as reading a law or contract and implementing one specific control.**

Compliance teams may need to interpret **regulations, contracts, standards, guidance, FAQs, and previous enforcement examples**.

They also need to combine these requirements with the organization's **risk assessment**.

When an organization does not fully meet an external requirement, the resulting **compliance risk** can be assessed using:

**Likelihood + Impact**

The likelihood depends partly on **how far the organization deviates from the requirement and how likely that deviation is to be discovered**.

The impact depends on what could happen if the non-compliance is discovered.

The compliance and risk teams then use this information to help management make decisions based on the organization's **regulatory risk tolerance**.
